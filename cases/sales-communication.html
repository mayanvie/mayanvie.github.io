case study

Supporting a sales team's communication skills

Needs analysis · Workshop design 4 hours · 20 learners Simulated portfolio piece

Role: L&D specialist (the only L&D function at the time)
Context: Fashion/beauty brand with e-commerce + a small showroom (simulated)

Portfolio note: This case is simulated — company, showroom, and conversations are constructed for the portfolio. The root cause (staff avoiding follow-up because it feels like pushing) comes from real observations across different work settings.
Learners

20 showroom staff for a fashion/beauty brand. They had product training and price-objection scripts, but in-store conversion lagged even though traffic was not bad.

Problem

Not just a confidence gap. Staff believed that explaining more when a customer said “it’s expensive” meant pushing product — so they knew the script and chose not to use it. A generic sales-skills workshop would miss that.

What I did

Scoped the need before agreeing to train. Proposed a 4-hour workshop, Beyond the Price Tag, plus a video brief, roleplay design, a job aid, and a 10-day coordination plan.

Design goal

Staff explain product value in their own words when price comes up — and can tell a pushy reply from one that helps the customer understand value.

1. Situation & my role

Simulated context: a fashion/beauty brand sold on e-commerce platforms and added a small showroom so customers could see products in person before buying online. Showroom sales were below expectation. Traffic wasn’t the problem — people came in — but same-visit conversion was low.

The showroom team lead assumed staff “didn’t know how to close” and proposed a sales-skills training right away. I was the only person covering L&D at the time. I was asked to look into the problem before agreeing to train. A generic session on “sales skills” sounds safe. It’s also easy to prescribe the wrong medicine.

2. Needs scoping

The first brief with the team lead gave me a starting picture: everyone had been through product training and had a script for price objections. On the floor, though, they often went quiet for a few seconds, answered hesitantly, laughed it off, or left the customer to decide alone. People usually walked out. The team lead guessed low confidence, or that they weren’t used to real pressure yet.

Before concluding, I asked for short conversations (10–15 minutes) with two showroom staff. Not to evaluate them — to understand what those moments actually felt like.

One detail stood out. A staff member said they knew the script, but every time a customer said “it’s expensive” and they tried to explain further, it felt a bit off. They worried the customer would think they were pushing product. So they usually stayed quiet, or said something light and stopped — to keep things comfortable for both sides.

That reframed the gap. It wasn’t only a confidence gap (unfamiliar pressure). There was also a belief gap: staff implicitly believed “explaining more when they say it’s expensive = pushing = I shouldn’t do that.” They had the script. They chose not to use it. That’s a different gap from a skill gap, and a workshop that only drills how to talk wouldn’t touch it.

3. Solution proposal

A skills-only session — practicing what to say when a customer says it’s expensive — would not be enough. The issue wasn’t that staff didn’t know what to say. They didn’t believe saying more was the right thing to do. So the solution still needed training, but it had to start with the belief before moving into skill.

I proposed one session: Beyond the Price Tag. Start by helping staff look at their role again — from pushing a sale to helping the customer understand value — then move into price-objection practice through realistic roleplay.

Alongside the workshop, a small extra support: where possible, give staff more access to how the product is made, or to material information, so believing in the price isn’t only theoretical.

4. Workshop outline: “Beyond the Price Tag”

Overview

  • Audience: 20 showroom staff
  • Duration: 4 hours
  • Location: at the showroom, after opening hours
  • Special role: the manager plays the customer in the roleplay

Learning objectives

  • Explain product value in their own words when a customer says it’s expensive — not only reread the script
  • Recognize the difference between a reply that pressures the customer and a reply that explains value

Part 1 — Reframe (90 min)

  • Open with a short video telling a real story about the product’s impact (brief in section 5)
  • Facilitator briefly introduces the core idea: pushing a sale vs. explaining value
  • Common situations and phrases — the group guesses which is which, then discusses

Part 2 — Roleplay practice (120 min)

  • Short Kahoot to review common price objections
  • 5–6 roleplay cases: manager as the customer; staff who’ve already shown strong responses go first
    • I pick people who’ve done well — not at random, and not the weakest person in order to “fix” them. It helps the person practicing feel recognized, and it gives the room a workable example to copy.
  • Debrief right after each case, before moving on — keep the insight fresh, so the next case repeats fewer of the same mistakes
  • Time left: staff bring real situations they’ve struggled with; the group brainstorms how to respond

Part 3 — Wrap-up (30 min)

  • No test. The session had just touched a real hesitation. A quiz right after would add pressure that works against the original goal.
  • A few light closing questions, for example:
    • What’s one thing you’ll try differently next time a customer says it’s expensive?
    • Which case felt most like something you’ve actually faced?
  • After the session: send a job-aid link (sample responses for common price objections)

5. Video brief: “Why This Price?”

Brief for the video team / production partner — used to open the “Beyond the Price Tag” workshop.

Video goal

Help staff feel product value in a real way — not through theory or slides. After watching, they should feel they’ve seen what’s behind the product, feel some inspiration about what they’re selling, and understand the price better.

Length: about 3 minutes
Tone: slow, grounded, real. Avoid ad-style polish and over-staging. Natural footage and voice over a perfectly finished look.

Content structure

Segment Content Purpose
Open A customer shakes their head at the price, then a quiet beat Start with the problem staff face every day, so the video is clearly about their situation
Main The people who make the product talk about their work, intercut with them working. Materials, why those choices, what they’re aiming for Show that “expensive” is deliberate effort and choice — not a random number
Close Another customer who has used the product and genuinely loves it — a short share Close the emotional arc: skepticism at the start → understanding and satisfaction at the end

Production notes

Keep the makers’ voices natural. They don’t need to read a finished script. Pauses and stumbles are part of the authenticity this video needs. Avoid background music and editing that looks like a typical ad.

Workstreams run in parallel, not strictly one after another. The video is the longest piece of work, so it sets the overall prep window. No separate dry-run block is needed.

Stakeholders

  • Showroom manager: plays the customer in roleplay — needs a brief on how hard the role should be
  • 20 showroom staff: learners — need advance notice of time and place
  • Internal video team: shoots and edits from the brief in section 5

7. Reflection & limitations

The company, the showroom, the team-lead brief, and the two staff conversations in this case are simulated. The root cause I landed on is not. Staff holding back from explaining more because it feels like pushing product is something I’ve seen repeat in different workplaces. This case builds a specific situation around that observation — not the other way around.

One choice I know is the safer, simpler one: replacing a factory visit with a video. Video is much easier to coordinate inside a simulated case. In a real project, I still think being on the floor where the product is made — seeing the work with your own eyes — would land deeper than any video, even if the logistics are heavier.

Wrap-up in this case is a few light questions. It doesn’t measure real effect. If this were live, I would look at conversion over a stretch of time and watch staff across several selling shifts — to see whether explaining value actually became more natural, or only felt good right after the workshop.
